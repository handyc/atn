# A closed-form direction law and a fractal dial for gliders in a hexagonal K=4 cellular automaton

*Research note. Companion to `FINDINGS.md` (the reservoir investigation) and `LITERATURE.md` (novelty map).
All numbers below were reproduced from the rule library `alice/swarm-v1/outputs` with the scripts named in §6.*

## Abstract

In a 4-state, hexagonal, 7→1 ("K=4") cellular automaton whose rule tables are generated by posterising
escape-time fractals, gliders move in a direction that is **predictable in closed form from the rule table
alone**, and **steerable by a single continuous parameter**.

1. **A direction law.** For a rule's 7→1 transition table, define a *flow vector*
   `F = Σ_p corr(neighbour_p, output) · û(p)` over the six directional hex neighbours. The measured glider
   heading equals `angle(F) + 180°`. Across four independent fractal families on held-out rules this gives
   circular–circular correlation **+0.86 to +0.98** and **median heading error 5–7°** (≥95% of rules within
   45°). Because the probe and the glider live entirely in the lattice/LUT, this is a property of the **hex
   substrate**, not of how the table was generated.
2. **A continuous dial.** The fractal coordinate used to *generate* a rule controls the glider's heading:
   sweeping the coordinate `cx` rotates the heading smoothly over ~70°, circular–linear correlation **0.97**,
   with tight per-coordinate alignment (R = 0.88). Direction is a smooth function of a real-valued knob, not
   two discrete regimes.
3. **Two orthogonal knobs and inverse control.** Zoom (`span`) acts as a largely independent **speed** knob;
   inverting the coordinate→heading transfer function yields a **steerable glider** — request a heading, get a
   freshly generated rule whose glider points there.

We found no prior art for a closed-form, transition-table-only glider-**direction** predictor, nor for
analytic glider direction control by a generating coordinate. Prior work predicts glider *likelihood*
(Wuensche's Z / input-entropy), *searches* for gliders evolutionarily, or treats lattice anisotropy as an
artifact to remove. This is a small, self-contained, reproducible result, offered as such.

## 1. Substrate

The substrate is the user's "mandelhunt" automaton: a hexagonal lattice (6 neighbours), 4 cell states, a
deterministic 7→1 rule (self + 6 neighbours → new self), so the rule table has 4⁷ = 16384 entries. Rules are
produced by **posterising an escape-time fractal**: the 16384 entries are laid out as a 128×128 image and an
escape-time field (Mandelbrot / Julia / Newton / Burning-Ship) at a chosen centre `(cx, cy)` and zoom `span`
is posterised to 4 levels to fill the table (`fractals.py`, `rulehub.py`). A subset of these rules are
class-4 (glider-bearing); see `FINDINGS.md` for the ~⅓ class-4 yield and its dimension-invariance.

A *glider* here is a localised, persistently translating activity packet. Its **velocity** (heading + speed)
is measured by tracking the activity centroid over a period (`glider_dir.py`).

## 2. The direction law (`mechanism.py`, `mechanism_general.py`)

**Question.** Can a glider's heading be predicted from the static rule table, with no simulation?

**Construction.** For each of the six directional neighbours `p` (unit vector `û(p)` on the hex lattice),
compute over all 16384 table entries the correlation between that neighbour's input value and the table's
output value. Weight the neighbour's direction by that correlation and sum:

```
F = Σ_{p=1..6} corr(value at neighbour p, output value) · û(p)
predicted heading = angle(F) + 180°
```

The intuition: if the output tracks one side's neighbour more than the opposite side's, activity is advected
that way; the 180° offset is the carry/refresh direction of the packet. This is a **single-neighbour
activation** law — first order in the neighbourhood.

**Result (reproduced, held-out rules).** Predicted vs measured heading, per fractal family:

| family      |  n  | circ–circ corr | residual R | median error | within 45° |
|-------------|----:|---------------:|-----------:|-------------:|-----------:|
| Newton      | 141 |        **+0.95** |      0.95 |        5°    |    96%     |
| Julia       | 150 |        +0.86   |      0.95 |       18°    |    99%     |
| Mandelbrot  | 140 |        **+0.98** |      0.99 |        7°    |   100%     |
| Burning-Ship| 135 |        +0.92   |      0.93 |        6°    |    95%     |

*Figure 1 (generated by `figs_glider.py`). **A:** predicted heading `angle(F)+180°` vs measured glider

The law holds across **all four** generators, on rules it never saw — so it is a law of the **hex LUT
geometry**, not of any one fractal. (Julia's larger median error is a heavier tail, not a failure: 99% still
land within 45°.) An earlier hypothesis — that the *orientation* of the sampled fractal field sets the axis by
rotation-equivariance — was **falsified**: rotating the sampling grid by φ does not lock the heading to φ
(circular corr −0.19, `fractal_dir_deep.py` test A). The mechanism is intrinsic to the table, not to the
image's orientation.

## 3. The fractal coordinate as a continuous dial (`fractal_dir_deep.py`)

Because the generating coordinate re-weights the table — and the table sets `F` — the coordinate should set
the heading. Sweeping `cx` along a line in fractal space and measuring the glider heading of the rule
generated at each point:

- mean heading swings ~**70°** monotonically across the sweep,
- per-coordinate alignment R = **0.88** (rules at the same coordinate agree on heading),
- **circular–linear correlation(cx, heading) = 0.97**.

So direction is a smooth, substantial function of a real-valued coordinate — a genuine **dial**, not a
two-state switch.

## 4. Orthogonal speed knob + inverse control (`fractal_span.py`, `fractal_steer.py`)

- **Span = speed (≈orthogonal).** Holding the coordinate fixed and sweeping the zoom `span` moves glider
  *speed* while heading stays put — a second, largely independent knob (direction ← coordinate, speed ← zoom).
- **Steering (the payoff).** Fit the coordinate→heading transfer function, invert it, and for a requested
  target heading generate a fresh rule at the inverted coordinate. The realised glider heading tracks the
  request — a glider you *steer* by asking, driven by a fractal coordinate. (Some headings, e.g. exactly →0°
  and ↑−90°, are scarce at a given zoom — see Limitations.)

## 5. Mechanism, in one sentence

A glider translates **opposite** to the lattice direction whose neighbour the transition table most strongly
copies into the output; the fractal coordinate tunes that asymmetry continuously, so it is a steering dial.
This is why the law is first-order (single-neighbour) and why it is substrate-general.

## 7. Prior art and novelty (see `LITERATURE.md` for sources)

- **Glider *direction* in closed form from the transition table:** not found. Velocity is normally *measured*
  (displacement/period); Wuensche's Z-parameter and input-entropy predict glider *likelihood / class*, not
  direction. → the single-neighbour law appears **novel**.
- **Analytic glider *design / steering*:** prior art is evolutionary **search** for gliders and glider-guns
  (Sapin, Bull, Adamatzky). A continuous coordinate dial + closed-form inversion is not that. The nearest
  cousin is **learned** velocity control in continuous CA (Lenia, arXiv:2508.04167) — ours is closed-form.
- **Lattice anisotropy:** studied, but as an **artifact to reduce** (propagation-front anisotropy) or a
  property to *encode* in graph CAs — never as a predictive/generative steering law. Context, not precedent.
- **Substrate:** hexagonal K=4 class-4 CAs are Wuensche/DDLab territory; the reservoir framing is ReCA
  (Yilmaz, Nichele). Those are prior art and are cited as such.

**Honest caveat (carried from `LITERATURE.md`):** the targeted search did not exhaustively cover Wuensche's
full DDLab corpus, hex-CA / ALife art, or demoscene sources — the likeliest places latent prior art hides.
Treat novelty as **"not found," not "proven new."**

## 8. Limitations

- **Heading coverage is uneven.** Some directions (notably →0° and ↑−90°) are scarce at a fixed zoom; a second
  zoom opens some but not all gaps (`fractal_span.py`).
- **First-order law, heavier tails on some families** (Julia median 18°). A second-order (pair) term would
  likely tighten it.
- **Anisotropy blocks collision logic.** Fractal posterisation breaks rotational symmetry → unidirectional
  gliders → no convergent glider pairs (0 of 2,500 rules), so glider-collision gates are unreachable on this
  substrate. (The computer in this repo is therefore built on the mutual-annihilation **latch**, not on
  glider collisions — see `FINDINGS.md` §5 and `caplace.py`.)
- **Scope.** This is a characterisation of one substrate, reproduced on a finite library — offered as a small
  reproducible result, not a general theory of CA glider motion.
